Qai Learns to Think

Qai is a small delivery robot on a university campus in Astana. Every week of the course it meets a new problem and has to learn a new way to think. This book follows its journal, from simple reflexes to probabilities, decisions, neural networks and large language models.

By Alikhan Ashimkhan · AI Fundamentals (AIF1303), QAIRU · 2026

How to read this book

Each chapter matches one week of the course. It opens with a page from Qai's journal (the problem), explains the idea in plain words, gives the exact definition or formula, works through one example with real numbers, and ends with a short quiz. Answers are hidden under each question. Three chapters contain small simulations you can run in the browser.

No prior AI knowledge is needed. Technical terms are explained the first time they appear and collected in the glossary.

01
Week 1 · Introduction to AI

A robot that only reacts

LOG W01 · DAY 1. Task: carry documents from Room A to Room B. My rules: if obstacle ahead, turn right; otherwise move forward. Result after 40 minutes: I walked around the same column 31 times. I never reached Room B.

Qai's first program is a simple reflex agent: it looks only at what it sees right now and applies an if-then rule. That works for a heater ("if temperature < 18 °C, turn on"), but not for a delivery. Qai has no memory of where it has been and no idea where Room B is.

What is an agent?

An agent is anything that perceives its environment through sensors and acts on it through actuators. A rational agent chooses the action that is expected to give the best result, according to a performance measure, given everything it has perceived so far.

Describe the task first: PEAS

PEASQai, the delivery robot
Performance measureDocuments delivered, time taken, no collisions, battery used
EnvironmentUniversity corridors, doors, people, rooms
ActuatorsWheels (forward, turn left, turn right, stop), gripper
SensorsDistance sensors, door detector, camera, wheel encoders

Classify the environment

Qai's world is partially observable (it cannot see around corners), stochastic (people move unpredictably), sequential (each move affects later ones), dynamic (the world changes while Qai thinks), continuous in space and multi-agent (students also walk there). A chessboard, by contrast, is fully observable, deterministic, static and discrete.

Five kinds of agents

Goal-basedWhich actions reach a goalFinds a route from Room A to Room B
Utility-basedHow good each outcome isChooses between a faster route and a safer route

AI and the law in Kazakhstan

Kazakhstan adopted a dedicated law "On Artificial Intelligence" (No. 230-VIII ZRK) on 17 November 2025. It is built around a few ideas that also apply to Qai:

  • Human first: human life, rights and autonomy come before efficiency.
  • Risk levels: minimal, medium and high risk systems need different controls.
  • Autonomy levels: does the AI only recommend, or does it decide alone?
  • Transparency: people must be told when a product or service uses AI, and synthetic AI content must be labelled.
  • Accountability: the owner of an AI system remains responsible for it.
Did you know?In 1950 Alan Turing proposed the "imitation game", now called the Turing test. The term "artificial intelligence" first appeared in the 1955 proposal for the 1956 Dartmouth summer workshop.
Mini-quiz
1. Which agent type does Qai need to remember an obstacle that is no longer visible?

A model-based agent. It keeps an internal state, so information survives after the percept disappears.

2. Is a crossword puzzle episodic or sequential?

Sequential. Every word you fill in constrains the later ones.

Week 2 · Uninformed search, A* and heuristics

Finding the way

LOG W02. New plan: before I move, I search the map in my head. I treat every corridor junction as a state and every move as an action. The question is no longer "what do I see?" but "which sequence of moves reaches the goal at the lowest cost?"

Formulating a search problem

A search problem has an initial state, a set of actions, a transition model (where each action leads), a goal test and a path cost. The algorithm explores a search tree: the frontier holds the states waiting to be expanded.

AlgorithmExpands firstShortest path?Memory
Breadth-first (BFS)The shallowest node (a queue)Yes, if every step costs the sameHigh
Depth-first (DFS)The deepest node (a stack)NoLow
Uniform-cost (UCS)The node with lowest path cost g(n)YesHigh
A*The node with lowest f(n) = g(n) + h(n)Yes, if h is admissibleHigh

The A* idea

Uninformed search does not know where the goal is, so it explores in all directions. A* adds a heuristic h(n), an estimate of the remaining cost to the goal:

f(n) = g(n) + h(n) g = cost so far, h = estimated cost to go

If h never overestimates the true remaining cost, it is admissible, and A* is guaranteed to find the cheapest path. On a grid where Qai moves up, down, left or right, the Manhattan distance |Δrow| + |Δcolumn| is admissible because walls can only make the real path longer.

Try it: A* vs breadth-first search

Click cells to add or remove walls. S is Qai, G is the goal.

Press a button to search.

Myth

A* always finds the shortest path.

Fact

Only when its heuristic is admissible (and, for graph search, consistent). A heuristic that overestimates can make A* return a longer path.
Did you know?A* was published in 1968 by Peter Hart, Nils Nilsson and Bertram Raphael at the Stanford Research Institute, for the Shakey robot.
Mini-quiz
1. A node has g = 5 and h = 3. What is f?

f = 8. A* will expand it before any node with f > 8.

2. Why is straight-line distance admissible for road maps?

No road can be shorter than the straight line between two points, so the estimate never exceeds the real cost.

03
Week 3 · Local search and constraint satisfaction

Climbing hills and keeping promises

LOG W03. Two new jobs. (1) Choose the best charging spot among many options: I only care about the final answer, not the path. (2) Help the dean's office place four exams into three days so that no student has two exams on the same day.

Local search

When only the final state matters, Qai can keep one current solution and improve it step by step. Hill climbing moves to the best neighbour as long as it is better. It is fast but can get stuck on a local maximum, a plateau or a ridge.

  • Random restart: run hill climbing many times from random starts and keep the best result.
  • Simulated annealing: sometimes accept a worse move, with probability e−Δ/T, where Δ is how much worse it is and T is a "temperature" that slowly decreases. Early on Qai explores; later it settles down.

Constraint satisfaction problems (CSP)

A CSP has variables, a domain of values for each, and constraints between them. The exam problem:

PartExam timetable
VariablesAI, Math, Programming, History
Domain{Mon, Tue, Wed}
ConstraintsAI ≠ Math, AI ≠ Programming, Math ≠ Programming, History ≠ AI (shared students)

Backtracking search assigns one variable at a time and goes back as soon as a constraint is broken. Three tricks make it much faster:

  • MRV (minimum remaining values): assign the variable with the fewest legal values first.
  • Forward checking: after AI = Mon, remove Mon from Math, Programming and History right away.
  • Arc consistency (AC-3): repeatedly remove values that have no compatible value in a neighbour.
Worked resultAI = Mon → Math = Tue → Programming = Wed (the only value left) → History = Tue. All four constraints hold, with no backtracking needed.
Did you know?Simulated annealing was introduced in 1983 by Kirkpatrick, Gelatt and Vecchi. It borrows its idea from metallurgy, where metal is cooled slowly so its atoms settle into a strong structure.
Mini-quiz
1. Why can hill climbing fail even if a better solution exists?

It only looks at neighbours. At a local maximum every neighbour is worse, so it stops, even though a higher peak exists elsewhere.

2. In the exam problem, after AI = Mon and Math = Tue, how many values are left for Programming?

One: Wed. It cannot share a day with AI or Math.

Week 4 · Adversarial search and game trees

When someone plays against you

LOG W04. A rival delivery company runs on the Astana–Almaty route. Whatever strategy we choose, they respond. I must assume they will respond in the way that hurts us most.

Minimax

In a two-player, zero-sum game, our agent is MAX and the opponent is MIN. Minimax computes values from the bottom of the tree: MIN nodes take the smallest child value, MAX nodes take the largest. It assumes the opponent plays perfectly.

Our strategy (MAX)Price cutNo changeFaster deliveryMIN picks
A: Low price8644
B: Standard3753
C: Express2911

Payoffs are invented profits in million KZT (a classroom example from Practical 4). The largest of the worst cases is 4, so MAX chooses A: Low price.

Alpha-beta pruning

α is the best value MAX is already guaranteed; β is the best value MIN is guaranteed. When a branch cannot change the decision, it is not explored.

Pruning never changes the result, only the amount of work. With good move ordering (best moves first) alpha-beta can search about twice as deep in the same time. Real games are too big to search to the end, so the search stops at a depth limit and uses an evaluation function, a weighted sum of features such as Eval(s) = w₁·f₁(s) + w₂·f₂(s).

Expectimax replaces MIN nodes with chance nodes (for dice or random events): their value is the probability-weighted average of the children.

Did you know?In 1997 IBM's Deep Blue defeated world chess champion Garry Kasparov in a six-game match. It used alpha-beta search on special hardware that evaluated around 200 million positions per second.
Mini-quiz
1. Does alpha-beta pruning ever give a different answer from minimax?

No. It returns exactly the same value; it only skips branches that cannot affect the decision.

2. A chance node has children 10 (probability 0.3) and 0 (probability 0.7). Its expectimax value?

0.3 · 10 + 0.7 · 0 = 3.

Week 5 · Markov decision processes and reinforcement learning

Deciding when the world is uncertain

LOG W05. My wheels slip on wet floors. When I command "forward", I sometimes end up somewhere else. A fixed plan breaks the first time I slip. I need a rule for every state: a policy.

The MDP model

A Markov decision process has states S, actions A, a transition model P(s′ | s, a), a reward R(s, a) and a discount factor γ between 0 and 1 that makes future rewards worth a little less than immediate ones. The answer is a policy π(s): which action to take in each state.

The Markov property says that the next state depends only on the current state and action, not on the whole history.

The Bellman equation and value iteration

V(s) = max over a [ R(s,a) + γ · Σ P(s′|s,a) · V(s′) ]

Value iteration starts with V = 0 everywhere and applies this update again and again until the values stop changing.

Worked example: a three-cell corridorCells: Start → Middle → Dock. Each move costs −1; entering the Dock gives +10 and ends the episode; γ = 0.9.
Iteration 1: V(Middle) = 10, V(Start) = −1 + 0.9 · 0 = −1.
Iteration 2: V(Start) = −1 + 0.9 · 10 = 8. The values no longer change, and the policy is "move right" in both cells.

Reinforcement learning: learning without the model

If Qai does not know P or R, it can learn from experience. Q-learning keeps a value Q(s, a) for every state and action and updates it after each step:

Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α [ r + γ · max Q(s′,a′) − Q(s,a) ]

Example: Q(Start, right) = 0, learning rate α = 0.5, reward r = −1, γ = 0.9 and max Q(Middle, ·) = 10. New value: 0 + 0.5 · (−1 + 9 − 0) = 4.

Qai must balance exploration (trying new actions) and exploitation (using what works). A common rule is ε-greedy: act randomly with a small probability ε, otherwise choose the best known action.

Did you know?Richard Bellman introduced dynamic programming in the 1950s. Q-learning was introduced by Chris Watkins in his 1989 PhD thesis.
Mini-quiz
1. What happens to an agent's behaviour if γ is close to 0?

It becomes short-sighted: only immediate rewards matter, and future rewards are almost ignored.

2. What is the difference between a plan and a policy?

A plan is a fixed sequence of actions. A policy gives an action for every state, so it still works after an unexpected outcome.

Week 6 · Probability and Bayesian networks

Believing the right amount

LOG W06. My hazard sensor beeped near the lab. The sensor catches 90% of real hazards. Does that mean there is a 90% chance of a hazard? I almost said yes. I was wrong.

Probability rules Qai needs

  • Conditional probability: P(A | B) = P(A ∧ B) / P(B).
  • Product rule: P(A ∧ B) = P(A | B) · P(B).
  • Independence: A and B are independent if P(A | B) = P(A).

Bayes' rule

P(H | E) = P(E | H) · P(H) / P(E)

P(H) is the prior (belief before evidence), P(E | H) is the likelihood (how well H explains the evidence), and P(H | E) is the posterior (belief after evidence). P(E) adds up every way the evidence can happen: P(E) = P(E | H)P(H) + P(E | not H)P(not H).

Try it: how much should Qai believe the alarm?

P(hazard | alarm) =
0.692

With the default numbers (from our Assignment 3 scenario), the answer is 0.18 / 0.26 = 0.692, not 0.9. Out of 100 situations, 20 are hazards and 18 of those raise an alarm, but 8 of the 80 safe situations also raise one. Of the 26 alarms, only 18 are real.

Bayesian networks

A Bayesian network is a directed acyclic graph: each node is a random variable, each arrow means "directly influences", and each node stores a conditional probability table given its parents. It describes the full joint distribution with far fewer numbers.

Once Qai knows whether the floor is wet, rain gives no extra information about slipping. "Slips" is conditionally independent of "Rain" given "Wet floor".

Myth

A sensor that is 90% accurate means a 90% chance that the alarm is real.

Fact

It depends on the base rate. When hazards are rare, many alarms are false, as the simulation above shows.
Did you know?Judea Pearl developed Bayesian networks in the 1980s and received the 2011 Turing Award for his work on probabilistic and causal reasoning.
Mini-quiz
1. Prior 0.5, P(E | H) = 0.8, P(E | not H) = 0.2. What is P(H | E)?

0.4 / (0.4 + 0.1) = 0.8.

2. Why must a Bayesian network have no cycles?

The joint probability is built by multiplying each node's probability given its parents; with a cycle a variable would depend on itself, and this factorisation would not define a valid distribution.

Week 7 · Hidden Markov models and sequential inference

Tracking what you cannot see

LOG W07. I lost GPS inside the warehouse. I am in zone A, B or C. A floor sensor shows RED or BLUE, but it is noisy. Each reading should update my belief, step by step.

The model

A hidden Markov model has a hidden state Xt (Qai's zone) that changes over time and an observation Et (the colour) that depends only on the current state. It needs three things: an initial belief b₀, a transition matrix P(Xt | Xt−1) and an emission matrix P(Et | Xt). Each row of both matrices sums to 1.

Filtering: predict → update → normalise

  1. Predict: b⁻ = b · T (where could I be after one move?).
  2. Update: multiply each zone by P(observation | zone).
  3. Normalise: divide by the sum so the beliefs add up to 1.
Worked step (Week 7 practice)b₀ = [0.5, 0.3, 0.2]. Predict: b⁻ = [0.41, 0.39, 0.20]. Observe RED, with P(RED | A, B, C) = [0.8, 0.5, 0.2]: weights [0.328, 0.195, 0.040], sum 0.563. Belief: [0.583, 0.346, 0.071], so Qai is most likely in A.

Try it: feed Qai sensor readings

Readings: none (initial belief)

Try the course sequence RED, BLUE, BLUE, RED. The final belief is [0.310, 0.506, 0.184]: zone B, even though the last reading is RED. The two BLUE readings pulled the belief towards C, and from C the robot cannot reach A in one step.

Other HMM tasks use the same model: smoothing (where was I at an earlier time?) and the Viterbi algorithm (what is the single most likely sequence of states?).

Did you know?HMMs were the core of most speech recognition systems from the 1980s until deep neural networks took over in the 2010s. The Viterbi algorithm was published by Andrew Viterbi in 1967.
Mini-quiz
1. Why do rows of a transition matrix sum to 1, but columns do not?

A row is the probability distribution of "where do I go from this state". A column mixes several distributions and need not sum to 1.

2. What does the sum before normalisation mean?

It is P(observation | all earlier observations), the probability of seeing this reading given the past.

Week 8 · Rational decisions and classical machine learning

From probabilities to actions

LOG W08. The campus café asked me to predict lunch demand. Knowing that tomorrow is probably busy is only half the job. They want to know: should they cook extra or not?

Maximum expected utility

A rational agent chooses the action with the highest expected utility (or the lowest expected loss): EU(a) = Σ P(outcome) · U(a, outcome).

Worked example: the café (Practice 8)Naive Bayes on a cold, rainy day gives P(High demand) = 20/22 = 90.9%. Losses: cooking Extra on a Low day = 60; cooking Usual on a High day = 30.
EL(Extra) = 2/22 · 60 = 5.45; EL(Usual) = 20/22 · 30 = 27.27. Choose Extra.

Prediction and decision are different steps: the loss table can change the best action even if the probability stays the same.

Supervised learning

The model learns a function from labelled examples. Classification predicts a category; regression predicts a number.

ModelIdeaCafé output
Naive BayesBayes' rule, assuming features are independent within a classP(High | cold, rain) = 90.9%
Linear regressionFit the line ŷ = wx + b with the smallest squared errorŷ = 0.246x + 4.2 → 48.5 lunches for 180 visitors
Logistic regressionLinear score passed through the sigmoid σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z)P(High | cold, rain) = 79.3%

Judging a model honestly

Data is split into training (learn the parameters), validation (choose settings) and test (final check on unseen data). A model that fits the training data perfectly but fails on new data is overfitting.

MetricFormulaCafé test (TP 21, FP 9, FN 3, TN 27)
Accuracy(TP + TN) / all80.0%
PrecisionTP / (TP + FP)70.0%

F12 · P · R / (P + R)77.8%

Myth

90% accuracy means a good model.

Fact

If 45 of 50 days are quiet, a model that always says "quiet" scores 90% accuracy and finds none of the busy days (recall 0%).
Mini-quiz
1. Why is Naive Bayes called "naive"?

It assumes all features are independent given the class, which is rarely true (cold and rain often come together), but it still works well in practice.

2. If cooking Extra on a quiet day cost 400 instead of 60, what should the café do at 90.9%?

EL(Extra) = 2/22 · 400 = 36.36 > 27.27, so Usual. The probability did not change; the cost of the mistake did.

Week 9 · Perceptrons and neural networks

Learning from examples, neuron by neuron

LOG W09. I need to tell stable floors from unstable ones using four sensor numbers. No one can write the exact rule. I will learn it from 18 labelled examples.

The perceptron

A perceptron multiplies each input by a weight, adds a bias and applies an activation function. The simplest activation is a step: output 1 if the sum is positive, otherwise 0.

Worked example: a perceptron for ANDWeights w₁ = 1, w₂ = 1, bias b = −1.5.
(0,0): −1.5 → 0 · (0,1): −0.5 → 0 · (1,0): −0.5 → 0 · (1,1): 0.5 → 1. This is exactly logical AND.

Learning means adjusting the weights after mistakes. The perceptron rule is w ← w + η (y − ŷ) x, where η is the learning rate. A single perceptron can only draw a straight line between classes, so it cannot learn XOR.

Multi-layer networks

A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) stacks layers of neurons with non-linear activations such as ReLU or sigmoid, so it can learn curved boundaries. It is trained with gradient descent: compute the error, use backpropagation to find how much each weight contributed, and move every weight a small step in the direction that reduces the error.

  • Learning rate too large: the error jumps around and may never settle.
  • Learning rate too small: training is very slow. In our Assignment 4, a rate of 0.0001 left the network under-trained, and it called 3 of 6 unstable floors "stable".
  • Network too large for little data: it may memorise the training set (overfitting).

Myth

100% test accuracy proves the model understands the problem.

Fact

In Assignment 4 a decision tree scored 100% using one feature only. When we raised just the grip value, it called all six dangerous floors "stable". High accuracy on a small test set can hide a shortcut.
Did you know?Frank Rosenblatt built the perceptron in 1958. In 1969 Minsky and Papert showed that a single layer cannot learn XOR, and in 1986 Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams popularised backpropagation for training multi-layer networks.
Mini-quiz
1. With w₁ = 1, w₂ = 1, which bias turns the AND perceptron into OR?

Any bias between −1 and 0, for example b = −0.5: (0,0) → −0.5 → 0, and every input with at least one 1 gives a positive sum.

2. Why do we standardise inputs before training a neural network?

So that all features are on a similar scale; gradient descent then converges faster and no feature dominates just because of its units.

Week 10 · LLMs, generative AI and AI safety

A robot that talks, and the rules it must follow

LOG W10. Students now ask me questions in plain language: "Where is room 304?" I received a language model. It answers fluently. Sometimes it answers fluently and wrongly.

How a large language model works

  • Tokens: text is split into small pieces (words or parts of words).
  • Next-token prediction: the model computes a probability for every possible next token and picks one, again and again.
  • Transformer and attention: the architecture behind modern LLMs (Vaswani et al., 2017). Attention lets every token weigh how relevant every other token is.
  • Training stages: pre-training on large amounts of text, then fine-tuning on instructions, often followed by learning from human feedback.

In other words, an LLM is a very large probabilistic model, built from the same ideas as earlier chapters: probability (Week 6), neural networks trained by gradient descent (Week 9) and, in feedback training, rewards (Week 5).

Limits and risks

These measures match the Kazakhstan AI Law from Chapter 1: people must know they are talking to AI, synthetic content must be labelled, and a human owner stays responsible.

If a chatbot sounds confident, it is probably right.

Fact

Confidence in wording comes from text patterns, not from checking facts. Important answers need a source or a human check.
Did you know?ChatGPT was released to the public on 30 November 2022. Its underlying idea, the Transformer, had been published five years earlier in a paper titled "Attention Is All You Need".
Mini-quiz
1. Why can an LLM produce a false fact with full confidence?

It generates the most probable continuation of the text; it does not check facts against a database unless one is connected.

2. Name one rule from the Kazakhstan AI Law that applies to a campus chatbot.

Users must be told that they are interacting with AI (transparency); the owner remains responsible for the system (accountability).

Glossary

Agent
Anything that perceives through sensors and acts through actuators.
Admissible heuristic
An estimate that never overestimates the true cost to the goal.
Alpha-beta pruning
Skipping game-tree branches that cannot change the minimax decision.
Bayes' rule
P(H | E) = P(E | H) P(H) / P(E): how to update a belief with evidence.
Bellman equation
The value of a state equals the best immediate reward plus the discounted value of what follows.
CSP
Constraint satisfaction problem: variables, domains and constraints.
Emission probability
In an HMM, the probability of an observation given a hidden state.
Expected utility
The probability-weighted average of the utilities of an action's outcomes.
Heuristic
A problem-specific estimate that guides search.
Hidden Markov model
A model of a hidden state that changes over time and produces noisy observations.
Learning rate
The size of each weight update during training.
MDP
Markov decision process: states, actions, transitions, rewards and a discount factor.
Overfitting
Fitting the training data so closely that performance on new data drops.
Policy
A rule that gives an action for every state.
Posterior
A belief after seeing the evidence.
Precision / recall
Share of predicted positives that are correct / share of real positives that are found.
Prior
A belief before seeing the evidence.
Token
A small piece of text that a language model reads and writes.
